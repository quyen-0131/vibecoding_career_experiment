# The Career Experiment — the case

## The problem

A third-year undergraduate has already chosen a major, and with it a set of
implied job titles. What they have not had is any way to find out whether they
want that work. They are asked to commit to a direction on the basis of course
descriptions, job adverts and what their friends are doing.

Where career counselling exists, it partly fills this gap. Where it does not —
which is most of the world, and most of Asia — students reach their final year
having thought about careers seriously for the first time in the year they are
supposed to be *consolidating* a choice, not starting one.

University is meant to be when you try things. Most students cannot: the
internships are few, the competition is heavy, and you cannot do four of them
to find out which one you liked.

## Why the existing answers do not work

**Careers quizzes** infer preference from personality and hand back a ranked
list of job titles. The output is a verdict the user cannot check.

**More information** — job descriptions, day-in-the-life videos, coffee chats —
gives a better picture of a job from the outside. It never tells you how the
work feels to do.

**Careers counsellors**, where available, give something genuinely useful: a
structured conversation. But the advice is a person's judgement, and it stays
general. Our users describe it as ambiguous.

All three answer the question "what jobs might suit someone like me?" None
answers "do I actually want to do this?" — which cannot be answered by
reading.

## What this product does

Three phases, for two careers at a time.

**1 — Find your existing evidence.** Read the user's history, extract the
activities in it, and make them confirm each one. A CV suggestion is not yet a
fact about them. Doing something is not evidence of enjoying it, and this
phase is careful never to imply otherwise.

**2 — Find your uncertainty.** For each group of activities, one question:
more, same, or less in your future work? Then an evidence map — what their
history covers for each career, and what it leaves untested.

**3 — Run an experiment.** Pick something untested. Do a bounded piece of the
real work of that role: structure the ambiguous problem, analyse the evidence,
write the recommendation. Get feedback. Revise. Then say how it felt.

## The thing only this product can produce

In phase 2, a user says "I want more analysis." That is an **imagined
preference** — a prediction about work they are recalling, not living.

In phase 3, they do analysis and report how it felt. That is an **informed
preference**.

The gap between those two answers is the product. "You thought you wanted more
analysis. After doing it, you were not sure." No quiz, no counsellor and no
amount of reading can generate that sentence, because it requires the person
to have done the work. It is direct evidence that experience beats
speculation, which is the entire thesis.

Where the work is something they have never done, there is no prediction to
compare against — and that is the other half of the value: the first real
datapoint they have ever had about it.

## What it refuses to do

The refusals are the product, not caveats on it.

- **No career-fit score, ever.** The moment a role name is printed as a
  conclusion, we are a quiz with better manners.
- **No verdict.** We answer "what should I explore first?", never "which
  career should I pick?" — a question one work sample cannot settle.
- **Performance never routes anyone.** The evaluation exists so the user has
  something real to react to. Someone who performs badly and enjoys the work
  is still pointed toward it. Weak work names a *skill to build*, never a
  reason to steer them elsewhere. A system that promised to measure enjoyment
  and quietly sorted on aptitude would be the one dishonesty this product
  cannot survive.
- **No claims about ability.** Users *have* activities; they *build* skills.
  We never infer the second from the first.

## What it gives back

A **direction**: the single most useful thing to explore next, and what to do
about it. Ranked by what would most distinguish the two careers — because an
unknown they both share teaches nothing about the choice — and broken ties by
what a student can realistically arrange next term.

Not "you should be a consultant." Rather: "you have never structured an
ambiguous problem, it is central to consulting and peripheral to product
management, and it is the fastest way to find out which way you lean. Here is
how to try it."

## Honest limits

- **Pay and market demand are out of scope**, and we say so to the user. Both
  matter enormously to a real decision. We answer what work you want; you must
  weigh earnings and hiring separately.
- **One session, not yet a loop.** The direction points at a next step, but
  nothing brings the user back with what they learned. The output is designed
  to be portable so a user can act on it; the return path is unbuilt.
- **Twelve careers.** Adding one costs roughly ten lines of structured prose —
  a context, five steps, four tasks, four rubric rows, activities and unknowns
  — because the scenarios are shared across every role. The marginal cost is
  nearly flat. The limit is authoring judgement about what a role really
  involves, not engineering.
- **An experiment is a work sample, not a job.** It generates evidence. It
  does not simulate a career, and we never claim it does.

## Why it should exist

The students who most need this are the ones with the least access to it. They
are not short of information about careers; they are short of *experience* of
them. This gives them a playground: somewhere to find out what the work is
actually like before committing years to it — and then one concrete thing to
do next.
